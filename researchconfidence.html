Confidence is Social Science Research           

[I wrote this for a student in one of the research methodology classes I taught for QU.]
(ddshinn@protonmail.com)

Dear Student:

Drawing conclusions from research data is a matter confidence:
Confidence that the sample was a "good" sample.
Confidence that the survey instrument was reliable and valid.
Confidence that the analysis found the "true" answer.

A "good" sample would collect data from the right population. If you want to know what QU students think about a topic - did you ask the students, or did you ask their professors or parents or..

A good sample would collect data from enough people. If you want to know what 900 UG full time undergraduate students at QU think, did you collect data from them all? That would be the best strategy but it is unlikely to be accomplished. So did you collect enough to have a representative sample? If you had truly random sample and collected data from all students that you sampled, you would need to collect data from about 250 students to be 95% confident that your results are true with a margin of error of +/-3%.

Whatever your sample is, the data are valuable. You just have to be honest about your confidence. It may be that the data you collect from a small sample are "true" for the whole population or it may be it's all wrong and biased in some way. The problem is we don't know. We attempt to create valid and reliable instruments and use statistics to give us an estimate of how confident we can be that the data are "true," as it were.

So a small sample provides you with data. You examine the data and draw conclusions. As a researcher, you then recognize and articulate the limitations e.g., your sample is small and the statistics warn you not to have too much confidence.

However, if, even with your small sample, the results are similar to what other researchers have found, then you can have greater confidence. Note I said confidence. I didn't say that you can be sure they are true. Only that you can have more confidence because the data are like other researchers' data.

As I said the other day, researchers don't prove anything. We ask research questions, form hypotheses, and answer those research questions. If we have done good research our limitations will be, excuse the play on words, our limitations will be limited. All research has limitations which decrease the confidence in the data to answer the research question. Sometimes you just have go with what you got and look for signs that you are correct or incorrect with the conclusions.

Practical applications to research is an art not a science. for instance, you may have heard that a small amount of aspirin every day will reduce the likelihood of a stroke or heart attack. The study yielded a correlation coefficient of .03. Really, really, small. Not a lot to have confidence in. Yet the study used over 20,000 people. The number of people increases our confidence there is something to .03. The .03 represents about 1500 people in the sample size of 20,000. For those 1500 people, their lives were saved because of the aspirin. So, the practical application is to recommend aspirin in part because even if the researchers are wrong, it's a small price to pay to be wrong.

So, consider the application or decision that will be made based upon your data. If it is an important decsion that you don't want to be wrong about (e.g., it could be costly in money or peoples' time and emotion), then you might want to have a lot of confidence. Is it a relatively insignificant decision that will be of little inconvenience if you are wrong, then you might not need a high level of confidence.
